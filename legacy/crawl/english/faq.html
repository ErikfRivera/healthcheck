F.A.Q


1. Who created the Health Check program?

The Health Check program was created by the Heart and Stroke Foundation of Canada in consultation with a dedicated group of experts from the fields of nutrition, education, medicine, communication and the grocery industry. We also consulted with Health Canada and the Canadian Food Inspection Agency.

2. What does the Health Check symbol mean when I see it on foods?

The Health Check symbol on food packages and restaurant menu items means the food has been evaluated by Heart and Stroke Foundation's dietitians to ensure it meets nutrient criteria which are based on Canada 's Food Guide.

3. How does a product or restaurant obtain the Health Check Symbol?

Food manufacturers or restaurants must submit a nutrient analysis of their product/menu item, which is reviewed by the Heart and Stroke Foundation's dietitians. If the product/menu item meets the nutrient criteria, a licensing agreement is signed and a modest annual fee is paid to help cover the cost of operating the not for profit/cost recovery program.

4. Is the Health Check program only for people with heart problems?

No. The Health Check program is intended for all Canadians who want help making healthy food choices. It is based on Canada 's Food Guide for people two years of age or older.

5. Why does Health Check consider sodium when evaluating products?

The Foundation's dietitians evaluate sodium values for all categories of foods as a way to help consumers make healthy choices. The Heart and Stroke Foundation recommends that most people, unless on a strict therapeutic diet, should limit their consumption of sodium to 2400 mg per day, which equals about 1 teaspoon a day.

6. What is the Health Check's criterion for sodium?

The Health Check criterion for sodium is based on the values from Health Canada's Heart Health Claim (480 mg for single foods such as crackers and 960 mg for entrées such as a frozen dinner/entree) which is meant to help reduce the risk of heart disease. To qualify for this claim, a food product must also meet other criteria besides sodium such as being low in saturated fat or contain 100 mg or less of cholesterol per 100g of food.

Sodium and different serving sizes

The nutrition criteria are evaluated against specific serving sizes of food. The serving size you see on food packaging varies by food category as defined by the Nutrition Labelling Act. Participating Health Check products are evaluated based on the serving sizes noted below and their labelled serving size.

Here are a few examples of foods, their sodium criterion and serving size:

  • Crackers : 480 mg or less / 50 g

  • Canned vegetables: 480 mg or less / 125 mL

  • Cooked meals: 960 mg or less / 250 g

  • Tomato or vegetable Juice: 650 mg / 250 mL

  • Soups: 650 mg / 250 mL

  • Nuts, seeds and dried fruit : No Added Salt

7. Why doesn't Health Check lower the sodium criteria?  

Health Check promotes healthy eating in general and is not meant to replace specific advice or treatments for those who must follow a therapeutic diet. Health Check has included upper limit values for sodium to assist Canadians to ‘limit their sodium intake' by using the criterion from Health Canada's ‘Heart Health Claim' (480 mg for single foods and 960 mg for entrées). Canada's Food Guide recommends limiting foods and beverages high in salt.

8. Do food manufactures and restaurants pay to participate in the Health Check program?

Yes. Health Check is a not-for-profit program that operates on a cost recovery basis. Food manufacturers/restaurants pay an annual fee for each product to cover the cost of running the program.

9. Do you test the food products/menu items to see if they continue to meet the Health Check program standards?

Yes. Products/menu items are subject to testing to ensure they still meet the Health Check nutrition criteria. This testing ensures the program's integrity.

10. What is Health Check's role in product quality?

The Health Check symbol on a package/menu means it is a healthy choice based on Canada 's Food Guide. The program guides consumers to healthy food choices. Individual food manufacturers or restaurants are responsible for quality, safety and taste. Questions about these topics should be directed to the individual food manufacturer or restaurant.

11. Who is responsible to ensure that food products/menu items are safe?

The Canadian Government, via Health Canada , sets the health and safety standards in Canada , while the Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) is there to enforce them. CFIA works with the food industry and consumer associations. This job requires the support of other levels of government such as the provinces and municipalities, who also play an important role.

For more information visit the following CFIA website: www.inspection.gc.ca

13. Why do some foods not display the Health Check symbol?

There may be a number of reasons why not all foods bear the Health Check symbol:

  • A company may be in the process of applying to the program.
  • The food may not meet the Health Check nutrient criteria.
  • A company may choose not to participate, even if the product/menu item could meet the criteria.

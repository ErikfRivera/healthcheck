  Learning from Food Labels

A food label can give you a snapshot of a food’s nutrition makeup in three ways. Here’s what to look for:

1.The Ingredient Listing: a quick glance tells you generally what’s in the food.

2.Nutrition Information: this gives you detailed nutrition information per serving. It can be very useful if you’re counting calories, cutting back on fat and sodium or trying to get more fibre. How many calories are there in a serving? Look on the label under ’Energy’ for both calorie counts and kilojoules.

Cutting back on fat and cholesterol? Look under ’Fat’. Although the different types of fat are given, ’total fat’ in grams is the most useful information. Use it to compare brands and make lower-fat choices. Also use it to size up the fat in a serving by comparing it to a healthy fat intake for a day. Without comparing to a daily guideline you might not realize that a frozen pot pie containing 25 grams of fat is a high-fat food.

Looking for more fibre? Look under ’Carbohydrates’. You’re making good choices if you get more than 2 grams of fibre per serving. Foods providing more than 6 grams are very high sources of fibre.

Trying to cut back on salt? Look for sodium in milligrams. As a general guideline, healthy adults should be getting no more than 3000-5000 milligrams of sodium daily. Less is better particularly if you’re on sodium restriction for high blood pressure.

So think twice about the canned soup that delivers 800-900 milligrams of sodium per cup!


3.Nutrition Claim: this highlights a food’s special nutrition features - usually in big or bold print, often on the front of a package. A claim gives you an instant bit of information - it’s all some people need to make better choices. But if you want more details, look under Nutrition Information (2) because once a claim is made, detailed Nutrition Information must also go on the label.
Ingredients you may want to avoid*: salt or other sodium-based ingredients such as sodium bicarbonate or monosodium glutamate; high-salt ingredients such as bouillon or soy sauce; hydrogenated vegetable oil or shortening; lard; butter; coconut oil.

*In small amounts these ingredients are not harmful and shouldn’t worry you. Trouble is, you can’t tell how much is present from an ingredient list. But some common sense will give you some clues. For instance, if several sources of sodium show up on the ingredient list, chances are this food is high in sodium. Or, if coconut oil is listed but you know the food is low in total fat, then you can be sure the amount of coconut oil is small.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What does the Health Check symbol mean when I see it on foods?
 
The Health Check symbol on food packages and restaurant menu items means that the food or menu item has met nutrient criteria developed by the Heart and Stroke Foundation's registered dietitians and a Technical Advisory Committee made up of nutrition experts based on recommendations in Canada's Food Guide. Food companies and restaurants are not involved in setting the criteria of the Health Check program.

2.  How exactly are Health Check grocery products and menu items evaluated?

Each participating product and menu item is evaluated against nutrient criteria developed by the Heart and Stroke Foundation’s registered dietitians based on recommendations in Canada’s Food Guide. The criteria include both nutrients people should limit such as total fat, saturated fat, trans fats, , sodium, and sugar, as well as nutrients we want to encourage people to consume more of such as fibre, calcium and vitamins and minerals.. A number of months ago we announced changes to our criteria which are summarized in the nutrient criteria section of this web site.

Random audits are carried out annually by an independent lab to make sure products and menu items continue to meet the nutrient criteria.

3. Does Health Check consider sodium/salt when evaluating products?
Yes. The Heart and Stroke Foundation's registered dietitians evaluate sodium values for all categories of grocery products and menu items as a way to help consumers make healthy choices. Reducing the amount of sodium in Canadians’ diets is one of the priorities of the Heart and Stroke Foundation and Health Check is committed to working with food companies and restaurant operators to reduce the amount of sodium in foods found in  our grocery stores and restaurants.
The Heart and Stroke Foundation was one of 17 leading health organizations in Canada to endorse a national Sodium Policy Statement developed by Blood Pressure Canada with the goal of reducing the daily sodium consumption of adult Canadians to between 1200  and 2300 mg by January 2020.  The Canadian Government estimates the average Canadian consumes more than 3100 mg of sodium daily, making the goal of between 1200 mg and 2300 mg a significant reduction.

4. What is Health Check doing to reduce sodium/salt in our diet?

Sodium reduction is a priority for the Heart and Stroke Foundation.  Health Check is actively working to reduce salt in products now, and has been since the program began. Our revised sodium criteria levels reflect this commitment.  A number of Health Check products already meet the revised criteria and restaurants are actively working on new menu items that will meet the new sodium criteria.  All companies well need to reduce their sodium levels to continue to participate in the Health Check program. Health Check plays a unique role in helping Canadians make healthy choices by working directly with restaurants and food manufacturers to reduce the sodium in our food supply.
 
5. What are Health Check's criteria for sodium/salt for restaurants? 

Changes t the sodium levels for large restaurant entrees were announced in December 2007  and must be in place by existing menu items by December 2009.  All new menu items entering the Health Check program must meet the lower sodium levels immediately.  The new sodium criteria is 960 mg which represents approximately a 25% reduction in sodium levels.  If a restaurant cannot meet the new criteria by the deadline, they must leave the Health Check program.The sodium criteria are different for each food and menu category. A summary of the criteria is available for the grocery program and for the restaurant program .
 
6.  Do companies pay to display the Health Check symbol?

No. Companies must earn the right to display the Health Check symbol by meeting the program’s nutrient criteria.  Only when a product or menu item meets our nutrient criteria the company pays a modest annual licensing fee to participate in the program. Health Check is run on a cost-recovery basis. All of the fees go towards the cost of running this independent, not-for-profit program.

7. Does the Heart and Stroke Foundation make money from the Health Check program?

No. The Health Check program does not raise funds for the Heart and Stroke Foundation. Health Check is a cost recovery, or non-profit program. Once a product meets the nutrient criteria, companies pay an annual licensing fee. All of these fees  are used to run the program and develop tools to educate consumers on healthy eating.   Participation in the Health Check program is independent of any fundraising the Foundation may conduct to  reduce the risk of heart disease and stroke. A number of Health Check companies do make separate donations to the Heart and Stroke Foundation to support the Foundation’s work around eliminating heart disease and stroke. These contributions are independent and have no connection with companies’ participation in Health Check.
 
8. Who created the Health Check program?

The Health Check program was created by the Heart and Stroke Foundation of Canada in consultation with a group of experts from the fields of nutrition, education, medicine, communication and industry. We also consulted with the federal government via Health Canada and the Canadian Food Inspection Agency.

9. Why did the Heart and Stroke Foundation develop Health Check?

Canadians were asking us for help in identifying healthy food products, especially in processed foods where the choices are harder, and told us the tools that were available were too confusing or insufficient to meet their needs. 

The Heart and Stroke Foundation chose to take a leadership role in developing Health Check as Canada's only independent third party symbol based on programs that were being run successfully by Heart Foundations in other countries. We recognized that, along with health professionals and nutrition experts, we needed to engage the food industry to develop healthier products or reformulate existing products and offer more healthy choices to Canadians.
 
10.  Why do some foods not display the Health Check symbol?
 
Health Check is a voluntary program open to all food companies and restaurants in Canada. Participants choose to have their products evaluated and be part of the program. There may be a number of reasons why foods may not bear the Health Check symbol:
  • The food may not meet the Health Check nutrient criteria.
  • A company may be in the process of applying to the program.
  • A company may choose not to participate, even if the product/menu item could meet the criteria.

11. Could products without the symbol be healthier than some Health Check products?

Health Check is a voluntary program, and we can only evaluate the products that are submitted to the Heart and Stroke Foundation.. Companies that choose not to participate in Health Check may meet our criteria, but participation is their choice. We encourage consumers to read the Nutrition Facts Table and compare products, and know that they can always use Health Check as a quick reference to say "this product fits within a balanced diet."

12. Is the Heart and Stroke Foundation interested in one national front of pack food program/symbol?

Yes. Consumers tell us that they are still confused and need help to make healthy choices, especially among processed foods. We believe that one national program based on the same principles as Health Check including science-based, transparent and objective criteria, would be a better approach. With one national program, only one standard set of criteria would be used and consumers could compare products more easily.

13. Are the food products/menu items tested to ensure they continue to meet the Health Check program nutrient criteria?

Yes. Products/menu items are subject to testing by an accredited lab to ensure they continually meet the Health Check nutrition criteria. This testing ensures the program's integrity.

14. Is the Health Check program only for people with heart problems?

No the program is meant to help healthy Canadians identify foods that can be part of an overall healthy diet. If you have specific dietary needs you should speak to your family physician.

 

15. Is Health Check an eating plan?

No. Health Check is a way of indentifying foods that can fit into a healthy diet based on Canada's Food Guide. You should eat a well balanced diet focusing on vegetables and fruit, whole grains and products that are lower in saturated and trans fat and sodium. Products in the Health Check program can be part of your overall diet.

16. Do the Health Check criteria address all ages?

Yes. The Health Check nutrient criteria are based on the Food Guide which includes nutrition recommendations for all Canadians aged 2 years and older.

17. Why are some products like French fries, muffins or cookie bars in the Health Check program?

Health Check offers a variety of additional choices for consumers in many different food categories. In categories like French fries, muffins or cookie bars as an example, some products provide more benefits that others within those categories. All Health Check participating products must meet specific criteria based on Canada’s Food Guide. Health Check has more than 1,800 participating products found in almost every aisle of the grocery store, and all can be included in a healthy eating plan based on Canada’s Food Guide. The Heart and Stroke Foundation supports a diet that includes foods from all four Food Groups:  Vegetables and Fruit, Grains, Meat and Alternatives and Milk Products.

18. Is the Health Check symbol on foods purchased by parents also intended for their children?
 
Yes. Health Check criteria are based on the Food Guide which is intended for Canadians aged 2 years and older. If these products meet our nutrient criteria, food companies can display the Health Check symbol.

19. Should I eat only Health Check foods?

Health Check is not a diet or eating plan - it's a way of identifying products that can fit into a healthy diet based on Canada's Food Guide.  You should eat a well balanced diet focusing on vegetables, whole grains and products that are lower in saturated and trans fat and sodium. Products in Health Check can be part of an overall healthy eating plan.

20. Does Health Check consider sugar when evaluating products?

Until recently, Health Check did not have sugar criteria, because there are no accepted scientific national or international limits we could use to establish criteria. But we know that Canadians are concerned about sugar in foods, and the latest Food Guide recommended limiting sugar intake, so Health Check has added sugar criteria that call for reductions of 25% for food categories noted in Canada's Food Guide as potentially higher sources of added sugar: cereals, muffins and snack breads, grain-based bars, cookies, and sorbet and fruit bars. These criteria will help us work with companies to reduce the sugar in foods that meet our other nutrient criteria.

21. Why do you have some snack products in your program?

The Heart and Stroke Foundation recognizes that most people enjoy variety in foods, and that snacks can be part of a healthy diet.  Health Check offers Canadians guidance in virtually all aisles of the grocery store, including snack options. Health Check includes more than 1800 participating products in up to 70 different food categories – and every product meets our criteria based on Canada's Food Guide.

22. Will I lose weight if I eat Health Check products?

Health Check is not a weight loss or calorie restriction program. It is a tool to identify foods that can fit within a healthy diet based on Canada's Food Guide.

23. What companies/products are in Health Check? 

For a complete listing of participating products go to: program participants .

24.  How does Health Check compare to other food information programs?  

There are a number of different programs with different package symbols. Some of these programs are run by private food companies for their own products and are based on different sets of nutrient criteria, so it's difficult to compare our program to theirs. Health Check is the only third party program in Canada that objectively evaluates any food that applies against Canada's Food Guide recommendations and approved Canadian nutrition claims, and is the only program in Canada that is publicly credited by senior health experts to improve the nutrition profiles of commercial foods.

25. What is Health Check's role in product quality and safety?

Individual food manufacturers or restaurants are responsible for food quality and safety. Questions should be directed to the individual food manufacturer or restaurant.  As is the case for all food products in Canada, foods that bear the Health Check symbol must comply with all government regulations.

26. Who is responsible for ensuring that food products/menu items are safe and for managing food recalls?

The Canadian Government, via Health Canada, sets health and safety standards in Canada, and the Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) enforces them. CFIA is responsible for managing all food recalls. CFIA works with the food industry and consumer associations.

For more information visit the CFIA website .
